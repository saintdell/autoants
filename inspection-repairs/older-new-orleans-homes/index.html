Common inspection items in older New Orleans homes

Shotguns, doubles, camelbacks and raised cottages have their own patterns. Here's what inspectors often write up in older New Orleans houses, which trade usually handles each, and what to ask. Your own report is what counts: these are patterns, not predictions.

Raised pier foundations

Many older houses sit on brick piers with wood sills and beams. Inspectors often note settled or leaning piers, gaps between a beam and a pier cap, and sloping floors. Structural and foundation contractors do this work, and inspectors sometimes ask for a structural engineer's evaluation first.

Ask: will the written scope say the method and which piers? If the inspector asked for an engineer, will an engineer's report be part of the job? Structural work on a home needs a Louisiana Residential Construction license.

Termites and wood damage

Formosan subterranean termites are established in New Orleans, so inspectors pay attention to mud tubes, damaged sills and framing. Treatment is done by licensed pest control companies (licensed by the Louisiana Department of Agriculture and Forestry); repairing damaged wood is a carpentry or structural job.

Ask: what is your LDAF license number? Will you give me a written wood-destroying insect report, and what does the treatment contract cover?

Older wiring

Older houses can have knob-and-tube wiring, two-prong or ungrounded outlets, older panels, and kitchens and baths without GFCI protection. Electricians handle these.

Ask: are you licensed as an electrical contractor by the City? Will the written scope list each circuit, panel or outlet?

Older pipes

Cast iron drain lines and galvanized supply pipes are common in older houses, along with water heaters near the end of their service life. Plumbers handle these.

Ask: what is your Louisiana State Plumbing Board license number? Will the scope say which fixtures and which runs of pipe?

Roofs, gutters and drainage

Inspectors note worn or damaged shingles, flashing at chimneys and walls, clogged gutters, downspouts that drain against the house, and soil that slopes toward it. Roofers, gutter installers and drainage contractors split this work.

Ask a roofer: residential roof work of $7,500 or more needs a Residential license with the Residential Roofing or Residential Construction class. Which do you hold?

Heating and cooling

Older systems, condensate lines, drain pans and attic air handlers come up often. HVAC contractors handle these.

Ask: do you hold the mechanical license this needs, and EPA Section 608 certification for refrigerant work?

Paint and historic districts

For a house built before 1978, federal rules require sellers to disclose known lead-based paint, and contractors who disturb painted surfaces should be EPA Lead-Safe (RRP) certified. If the house is in a local historic district, exterior changes such as windows, doors and roofing materials may need Historic District Landmarks Commission review before work starts.

Ask a painter: are you EPA Lead-Safe certified? Ask anyone doing exterior work: if the house is in a local historic district, will you get the approvals the work needs?

What these jobs are declared at on permits

From City of New Orleans permit filings (applications 2024 to 2026, in 2026 dollars), the middle half of declared values for whole jobs:

  • Re-roofs: $12,200 to $23,800
  • HVAC jobs: $7,800 to $16,600
  • Water-heater installs: $770 to $3,600
  • Electrical jobs: $2,100 to $11,500

Public-permit declared values: what owners and contractors wrote on City permit filings for whole jobs of each kind (data.nola.gov, Permits - BLDS, CC0; adjusted with BLS CPI-U; read 2026-10-10).
